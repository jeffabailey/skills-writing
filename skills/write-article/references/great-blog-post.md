Cross-cutting quality reference for any blog post, whatever framework drives its structure. Read it while drafting and again while revising. It distills the advice that applies to blog posts from Ann Handley, *Everybody Writes: Your Go-To Guide to Creating Ridiculously Good Content* (John Wiley & Sons, 2014, first edition). Everything below is paraphrased from the book unless quoted. Where the book and the site's `writing-style.md` disagree, the site guide wins (see the last section).

## The quality bar

Handley's test for content multiplies rather than adds: "Utility × Inspiration × Empathy = Quality Content." If any factor is zero, the post is worth zero.

* **Utility.** The post helps the reader do something that matters to them: solve a problem, ease a burden, make a decision.
* **Inspiration.** It rests on data or a fresh creative angle, and it reads as if only this author could have written it.
* **Empathy.** Every choice is made from the reader's side of the page.

She also says writing, more than timing, graphics, or frequency, decides whether a post works. She quotes Guy Kawasaki: "Writing is the primary determinant of the success of the post." Treat publishing as a privilege granted by the reader's attention, and keep in mind that nobody has to read this.

## Think before you write

When a draft stalls, the thinking is usually the problem. Before drafting, answer three questions and keep the answers at the top of the working file:

* **Objective.** Why does this post exist? What should it achieve? The author has to care about it, because readers detect insincerity.
* **Point of view.** What is the author's actual take? State it in one plain sentence. That sentence often becomes the headline.
* **So what?** Ask why the reader should care, answer with "because," and repeat until the answers run out. The last useful answer is the real message. Rewrite the post's main idea around it.

If the author thinks by writing, that is fine. The point is a rough sense of direction and of the reader, not a finished argument.

## The writing process

The book lays out a 12-step process that Handley uses for her own blog posts. Steps can move around, but none should be skipped:

1. Set the goal.
2. Reframe it for the reader (the so-what exercise above).
3. Gather data, examples, and stories. The author's own experience counts as a source but should not be the only one.
4. Choose a structure and outline it.
5. Picture one specific reader and write to that person.
6. Write the ugly first draft.
7. Step away, ideally overnight; at minimum, take a break.
8. Rewrite from the reader's seat.
9. Write the headline.
10. Get an edit from someone else.
11. Check readability: scannable, short paragraphs, subheads, lists.
12. Publish, having answered "what should the reader do now?"

**The ugly first draft.** Expect the first draft to be bad. Get the ideas down in any order, ignore grammar, and leave placeholders such as "need a stronger example here" or "find a stat." Good writers are usually bad first drafters and strong self-editors. Handley notes that many bloggers stop at this stage; a great post does not.

**Write to one person.** If the opening will not come, picture a real, liked reader and start as if writing them a letter. Addressing someone specific relaxes the voice into a conversational one.

**Use deadlines.** Polishing has no natural end. Set a hard deadline, do the best work possible by then, and call it finished.

## Rewriting is where the quality comes from

Handley's position is that the best lines, analogies, and humor all arrive on the rewrite. She splits self-editing into two passes, done in order.

**Big-picture pass (she calls it editing by chainsaw).**

* State the key idea clearly near the start.
* Cut anything that does not support the main point, even a good anecdote.
* Make every paragraph add an idea its neighbors do not, and make its sentences build on one another.
* Make every sentence add something new; delete restatements.
* Reorder sections until each point leads into the next.

**Line pass (editing with surgical tools).**

* Trim bloat and words that state the obvious ("in this post," "I've always felt that").
* Swap wordy phrases for single words: "although" for "despite the fact that," "most" for "the majority of," "to" for "in order to," "remains" for "continues to be."
* If "in terms of" appears, the thinking is fuzzy; recast the sentence.
* Replace weak verbs with precise ones, without overdoing it.
* Keep an adverb only when removing it changes the meaning.
* Prefer active voice.
* Make sure "this," "that," and "those" point at an unmistakable antecedent.
* Build transitions by carrying an idea from one paragraph into the next, not by leaning on "however" or "therefore."

**Swap places with the reader.** On the rewrite, read as a skeptic. Ask what experience the post creates, what questions the reader will have at each point, and whether the reader is working too hard to follow. Answer those questions in the text. The reader is the only audience to please, not a boss or a client. Don Murray, quoted in the book: "The reader doesn't turn the page because of a hunger to applaud."

## Structure, openings, and endings

**Pick a shape on purpose.** Good writing has a logic the reader can feel. The book offers many angles for the same topic: explainer, how-to, how not to, first-person story, case study, contrarian take, comparison, data story, Q&A, roundup of expert views. Choose the one that fits the point, then outline.

**Cut the running start.** Writers warm up with background before reaching the point. Draft it if it helps, then delete it. Try removing the first paragraph or two; the post usually improves.

**Put important words first.** Open sentences with the subject and the action. Move throat-clearing such as "According to," "There is," "It is important to," "In my opinion," "I think," and "The purpose of this post is" to the end of the sentence, or drop it.

**Give the opening extra care.** The book's options for an opening:

* Put the reader, or someone like them, into a scene.
* Describe a problem the reader recognizes.
* Set the stage with surprising context.
* Lead with a startling, credible number.
* Tell a short personal story, ideally ending on a hook that raises a question.
* Ask a question, but rarely, because it quickly sounds like an infomercial.

**Finish strong.** The ending is second only to the opening in importance. Avoid trailing off, and avoid the lazy "so what do you think?" Better options are restating the biggest idea as a fresh synthesis (not a repeat), shifting tone for a surprising final line, or giving a quoted source the last word. Then tell the reader what to do next.

## Clarity and brevity

**Brevity means cutting fat.** "Take as long as you need to tell the story." Cut the parts that serve the writer and waste the reader's time, and keep whatever the story needs.

**Simple, not simplistic.** Explain as if the reader knows nothing about the topic, while respecting their intelligence. Clear writing comes from clear thinking. Consider whether a chart or diagram would carry an idea better than words.

**Readable on screen.** The book's guidelines: paragraphs of about three sentences or fewer (one is fine), sentences under roughly 25 words, plain words over jargon (unless the audience uses the jargon), subheads, lists, and plenty of white space. Readability scores such as Flesch-Kincaid are a rough check, not a substitute for judgment.

**Real words only.** Cut buzzwords ("leverage," "best-of-breed," "cutting-edge"), stitched-together coinages, nouns forced into verbs ("incentivize," "learnings"), and tech jargon applied to people ("bandwidth," "ping," "on my radar"). Use a cliché only when it is a genuinely useful shorthand.

**Don't preach.** Lines that open with "Don't forget," "Always remember to," or "Never" can sound condescending. Instruct without lecturing.

## Voice and storytelling

**Tell the story only you can tell.** Bland, interchangeable copy could describe anyone. Handley's test: hide the name and the logo, and ask whether readers would still know who wrote it. Lean on the author's specific experience and perspective. The point has probably been made before; say it better.

**Voice stays the same; tone adapts.** Voice is consistent personality. Tone shifts with the reader's situation; a joke that works in one post can grate in a post about something frustrating.

**Tell a true story well.** Stories should be true, human, and original, and should serve the reader. Put real people, real situations, and real emotion on the page. The reader, not the author or the product, is the hero.

**Show, don't tell.** Show how the idea plays out in someone's life rather than asserting that it matters. Ask what pain the topic removes and what better state it creates.

**Specificity.** Name things: the breed, not "dog"; the person, not "a client"; the number, not "a lot." The book's journalism-school rule: "Be specific enough to be believable, but universal enough to be credible."

**Analogies that help.** Use a comparison built from something the reader already knows, with a little surprise in it. Turn abstract numbers into tangible ones. Avoid worn comparisons.

**Teach.** Don't just state what the author feels or what works; explain why, and what led there.

**Look for content moments.** There is always a story, even in a dull subject. Use what is ordinary to the author and interesting to others, draw on events outside the field, and borrow approaches from other fields instead of waiting for an example from your own.

## Evidence and credibility

**Data before declaration.** Handley's phrase. If you tell readers what you think, give them the evidence: research, what others have said, and what supports or complicates the claim.

**Tell the whole truth.** Credit sources, ground claims in data, admit bias, disclose conflicts and connections, and keep anonymous sources to a minimum. Include perspectives beyond the author's own.

**Biased and balanced.** Take a clear side and defend it, but fight fair: acknowledge the facts and the strongest counterargument, then explain why the position still holds. Don't take cheap shots at named rivals.

**Fact-check everything.** Question every statistic, number, and bold claim, and confirm it can be backed up. Check the spelling of names, products, and companies, keep them consistent, and click every link. One error undermines the whole post.

**Cite as you write.** Record where each idea, quote, and number came from while drafting; ideas are easy to absorb and later mistake for your own. Go to the primary source, not someone summarizing it. Favor recent research (the book suggests under four years old, under two in fast-moving fields). Treat Wikipedia as a lead to sources, not as a source. Credit the people who inspired an idea, not only those quoted.

**Best sources and hidden agendas.** Quote the person closest to the facts, not a spokesperson. Know what a source stands to gain, and disclose it if you use them.

**Curate with a point of view.** When building on someone else's work, add context and a take, keep your own words longer than any quoted passage, quote briefly, and credit the original openly. Get permission before reusing images.

## Headlines

Spend real time on the headline; it tells readers what the post delivers and why to keep reading. Promise exactly what the post delivers, as specifically as possible, and put the reader's benefit in it. Curiosity is fine only when the payoff lives up to it; hype that overpromises erodes trust. Numbers set expectations. Use a vivid adjective only when it is literally true. The book suggests keeping headlines short enough to avoid truncation, but treats specificity as more important than length.

## What the book says about blog posts specifically

* Writing quality comes first; tactics are secondary.
* Keep it tight. The book cites about 1,500 words as a search sweet spot, while warning against padding and endorsing much shorter posts when they are useful. Kawasaki prefers under 1,000.
* Structure for scanning: subheads, short paragraphs, bulleted and numbered lists.
* Every post needs a strong visual, not a cliché stock photo.
* Give the reader a clear next step.
* Choose an interesting angle (see Structure).
* Publish consistently, write for the audience's audience as well as the audience, and experiment.
* Don't dress up minor internal news as a post. Use Donald Murray's test: would a reader turn to someone and say "listen to this"?

## Where site rules win

The site's `writing-style.md` takes precedence over the book in these places:

* **Punctuation.** The book uses em dashes freely; the site bans them.
* **Fragments and one-line paragraphs.** The book allows sentence fragments and a single sentence set apart for emphasis. The site bans fragmented pseudo-profound sentences and line breaks used for weight. Keep one-sentence paragraphs rare and plain.
* **Emphasis.** The book suggests bolding or italicizing key points and using pull quotes for scanning. The site treats emphasis as subtractive: bold only for short lead-in labels or true warnings, at most two or three bold or italic runs per section.
* **Paragraph length.** Follow the site's 2 to 4 sentences over the book's 3-or-fewer.
* **Point of view.** The book pushes "you" over "I/we" and allows "we" in brand voice. The site uses first person "I" for essays and opinion, imperative and "you" for Diátaxis pieces, and never "we" or "our."
* **Moralizing.** The book discourages "Don't" and "Never." Diátaxis how-tos and tutorials still use plain imperatives as the site requires; just avoid a scolding tone.
* **Endings and calls to action.** The book's next steps include subscribing, registering, or buying. The site wants concrete next steps or takeaways, no sales pitch, and no hollow engagement prompts.
* **Headlines.** The book's lively adjectives (ultimate, brilliant, awesome) and curiosity gaps clash with the site's ban on hype. Title length and SEO mechanics follow `seo-front-matter.md`.
* **Citations and links.** The book suggests naming a source in passing with a direct link. The site requires reference-style Markdown links defined once, a `## References` section for factual claims, and no inline or HTML links.
* **Visuals.** The book wants a large image on every post. The site handles covers through its own pipeline, uses Mermaid diagrams in place of arrow text, and replaces tables with the cards shortcode.
* **Humor.** The book encourages humor wherever it fits. The site says use it sparingly, only where it sharpens the point.

## Pre-publish checklist

Run this against the draft. Each item should be answerable yes or no.

* [ ] The main point fits in one sentence, and the post says it clearly near the top.
* [ ] The so-what is answered: the reader can tell within the first few paragraphs why this matters to them.
* [ ] The opening skips warm-up background and puts the reader or a concrete situation on the page quickly.
* [ ] Every section, paragraph, and sentence adds something new; restatements and off-point anecdotes are gone.
* [ ] The structure is deliberate and each section leads into the next with a real transition.
* [ ] The ending synthesizes or lands a final idea and gives a concrete next step, with no "what do you think?" filler.
* [ ] Claims are specific: names, numbers, and examples instead of "a lot," "solution," or "some."
* [ ] Every opinion comes with the reason and evidence behind it, and the strongest counterpoint is acknowledged.
* [ ] Every statistic and claim traces to a primary, reasonably recent source cited in the site's link style.
* [ ] Names, product spellings, and links are checked and consistent.
* [ ] No buzzwords, forced verbs, tech jargon about people, or reflexive clichés.
* [ ] Sentence openings lead with the subject and action, not "There is," "It is important," or "I think."
* [ ] Wordy phrases are tightened, adverbs justify themselves, and passive voice is used only on purpose.
* [ ] Paragraphs are short, subheads and lists aid scanning, and no sentence runs much past 25 words without reason.
* [ ] The headline promises exactly what the post delivers, specifically and without hype.
* [ ] The post sounds like this author and could not be mistaken for anyone else's.
* [ ] The draft got a step-away and a rewrite from the reader's seat, not just a proofread.
* [ ] It complies with `writing-style.md` (no em dashes, no banned phrases, emphasis restraint) and `seo-front-matter.md`.
